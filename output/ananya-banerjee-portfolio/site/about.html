The long versionAnanya Banerjee

I started with mathematics. I kept following the questions.

Somewhere along the way, equations became algorithms, algorithms became AI systems, and curiosity started turning into products, games, communities and stories. I didn't plan most of it. I just kept following whatever I couldn't stop thinking about.

Chapter 01BITS Pilani · Mathematics

I liked patterns before I knew I'd build systems.

Mathematics taught me to look beneath the obvious answer. To search for structure. Patterns. The rules hiding underneath things that looked, at first glance, like they didn't have any.

I didn't know it then, but that instinct would follow me everywhere — into code, into products, into the way I still approach a problem I don't understand yet.

M.Sc. (Hons) Mathematics Probability & Statistics · Optimization Linear Algebra · Graphs & Networks · Topology

Mathematics → Intelligence↓

Chapter 02UT Dallas · Intelligent Systems

Then I discovered machines could learn the patterns too.

Computer science gave mathematics somewhere to go. Machine learning, natural language processing, and artificial intelligence turned abstract patterns into systems that could actually predict, respond, and behave.

That changed the questions I wanted to ask — from "what is the pattern?" to "what can I build that uses it?"

M.S. Computer Science — Intelligent Systems NLP · Machine Learning · AI Jonsson Scholar

Intelligence → Inquiry↓

Chapter 03Research · AI Institute · DLRL

I stopped just studying AI and started asking it questions.

Research changed my relationship with technology. Instead of only asking "how does this work?" — as a research intern in artificial intelligence, developing a new approach to object detection — I started asking "what hasn't worked yet?"

That question turned out to be addictive. It's the same one I still ask before I start almost anything.

Inquiry → Impact↓

Chapter 04Software Engineering · eBay

Then I learned that shipping changes how you think.

Research is a controlled environment. A real product isn't. At eBay, I worked with cross-functional teams across product, development, and design — designing and shipping complex, multi-tier distributed systems end-to-end, for real users, at real scale, on real deadlines.

Elegant ideas are only the beginning. Real systems have to survive reality.

Constraints, tradeoffs, reliability requirements — none of that shows up in a research paper. It only shows up once something is live.

A turning point

Something changed when I started building from zero.

Hackathons gave me a different kind of engineering problem. No inherited roadmap. No established system. Just an idea and one question: can we make this real?

That feeling stayed with me. It's the bridge between being a software engineer and becoming an independent product builder.

Somewhere along the way, "I have an idea" quietly became "I wonder how quickly I can ship this."

Chapter 06DramaBubble Reviews · Writing · Podcast

Not every obsession became code.

Sometimes curiosity became writing. Sometimes it became a podcast. And sometimes watching far too many Asian dramas somehow became an entire media brand — DramaBubble Reviews, now a review website with its own Instagram, YouTube, and LinkedIn channels.

It sounds unrelated to AI agents and distributed systems. It isn't. It's the same instinct — notice something, follow it further than is reasonable, make something out of it — just pointed at stories instead of software.

Independence → Agency↓

Chapter 07AI Agents · LinkedIn · Now

And now, I'm back inside one of the questions that fascinates me most.

What happens when software can reason and act?

At LinkedIn, that question is the job: AI agents, agent systems, LLMs, harness engineering, reliability, human-agent interaction — the systems that sit around a model and decide whether it can actually be trusted to do something.

It feels like the early interests returning at a different level. Mathematics asked "what's the pattern?" AI asked "can a machine find it?" Now the question is "can a machine act on it, reliably, in the real world?" Same curiosity. Different altitude.

Closing thought

I don't think the point is to pick one box.

Engineer. Builder. Writer. Creator.

I've spent enough time wondering which one is the "real" one. The answer is probably all of them. The medium keeps changing. The instinct doesn't: notice something, question it, make something, share what I learned.

What comes next? I don't know exactly.

That's part of what makes it interesting.
